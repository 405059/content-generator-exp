Child, come sit down. Let me tell you about something that happened to me over thirty years ago. Back then, I had just become the workshop director. I thought I was pretty amazing, managing over twenty workers, walking around with a spring in my step. It was the summer of 1982, and the factory had received a large order—we needed to produce five thousand radios within a month. I calculated the time and realized that at our normal pace, we definitely couldn't finish. So I thought about having the workers work overtime, running three shifts around the clock. I gathered everyone together and said confidently, patting my chest: "Brothers, let's give it our all this time—double bonuses!" The workers were all excited and worked until two in the morning on the first day. But by the third day, problems started to appear. Old Wang—the most experienced master craftsman in our workshop—came to me, even stuttering a bit as he spoke: "Director, this won't work. Everyone is exhausted, their hands are shaking. Yesterday alone, several machines had parts installed incorrectly." I lost my temper right then and there. I thought, how could Old Wang be so tactless? So I scolded him: "Old Wang, what do you mean by this? If you're afraid of hard work, go home and rest! Can't even handle this little bit of hardship, and you still want bonuses?" Old Wang's face turned red after my scolding, but he still insisted: "Director, I'm not afraid of hard work. There really is a problem." I waved my hand and sent him away. The next morning, as soon as I arrived at the workshop, I heard a loud "bang." I rushed over to take a look, and my God—Little Li, because he was too tired while operating the stamping machine, his hand slipped, and he nearly crushed his fingers! Although it ended up being just a superficial injury, it scared everyone badly. What's worse, when I inspected the products from the past few days, I discovered that nearly three hundred radios had quality issues—some had wiring connected incorrectly, some had crooked casings, and some simply didn't work at all. Only then did I realize that Old Wang was right—everyone really was over-exhausted. I immediately stopped the overtime and gathered everyone together. I looked at Old Wang, and in his eyes there was no blame, only concern. In front of everyone, I apologized to Old Wang: "Master Wang, I'm sorry. I was too impatient and didn't listen to you." Then I rearranged the work schedule, letting everyone work normal hours, but raising our efficiency and quality standards. And you know what happened? In the end, not only did we complete the order on time, but the quality was even better than before—not a single unit needed repairs. The factory director praised our workshop, saying it was the finest batch of products he'd ever seen. Child, from that moment on, I understood one principle: True leadership isn't about making everyone listen to you, but about learning to listen to others. Sometimes, the most valuable advice comes from those you think are "singing a different tune." Young people always want to achieve results quickly, to prove themselves fast, but remember—impatience is the biggest enemy on the road to success, and humility is the best teacher.
